# Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

## One-sentence definition
> Hereditary traits are passed from parents to offspring as discrete units (genes) that segregate and assort independently according to simple statistical rules.

## Full explanation
Mendel’s three laws (modern formulation):
1. **Law of Segregation**: Each individual has two alleles for a trait; these alleles separate during gamete formation so each gamete receives only one.
2. **Law of Independent Assortment**: Alleles of different genes assort independently of one another during gamete formation (true for genes on different chromosomes).
3. **Law of Dominance**: In a heterozygote, one allele may mask the expression of the other.

These laws provided the missing mechanism of inheritance that Darwin’s theory needed.

## Feynman-style explanation
Your parents each have two recipe cards for every trait. When they make you, they each randomly give you one card. If one card is “stronger” (dominant), that is the recipe you follow, but you still carry the other card and can pass it to your own children. Different traits are usually shuffled independently, like dealing from separate decks.

## Key metaphors & analogies

1. **Recipe cards / instruction booklets** — Discrete packets of instructions that are copied and passed on.
2. **Coin flips** — Segregation is like flipping a coin for which allele a gamete receives.
3. **Two decks of cards shuffled separately** — Independent assortment.

## Concrete examples

1. **Pea plant flower color** — Crossing pure purple with pure white yields all purple F1; F2 shows 3:1 purple:white.
2. **Human ABO blood types** — Multiple alleles and codominance still follow Mendelian transmission rules.
3. **Cystic fibrosis** — Recessive Mendelian disease; carriers are heterozygous and usually unaffected.

## Quantitative / data anchors
- Classic monohybrid ratio: 3:1 phenotypic
- Dihybrid ratio: 9:3:3:1
- Punnett square probabilities

## Historical & discovery context
Mendel’s experiments on pea plants (1856–1863) were published in 1865 but largely ignored until rediscovered in 1900 by de Vries, Correns, and von Tschermak. They became the foundation of classical genetics.

## Common misconceptions
- “All traits follow simple Mendelian ratios.” → Many traits are polygenic, epistatic, or influenced by environment.
- “Dominant means better or more common.” → Dominance refers only to phenotypic expression in heterozygotes.
